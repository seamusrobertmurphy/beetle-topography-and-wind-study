## Question 1, landscape predictors and refugia

Established methods. The landscape models of where the outbreak went are logistic or
autologistic regressions of aerial-survey or Landsat attack on a grid, with a term for
attack in the previous year or in the neighbourhood, and climate, terrain and stand
attributes beside it. Aukema et al. (2006) fitted an autologistic regression with a
first-order spatial neighbourhood term on 12 km cells across British Columbia, and Aukema
et al. (2008) found "the presence of outbreaking populations was highly correlated with
outbreaking populations within the nearest 18 km the same year and local populations
within 6 km in the previous two years" (p. 348). Wulder et al. (2006) fitted red attack at
30 m in Montana on a wetness difference, elevation and slope, with radiation dropped by
forward selection, and found "Per unit changes in EWDI or elevation result in an increase
of 11% and 0.7%, respectively, in the odds of red-attack damage. Conversely, increases in
slope result in a decrease in the odds of red-attack damage by 15.64%" (p. 159), and read
the elevation sign as the host's distribution along ridge tops rather than the beetle's
preference. Walter and Platt (2013) fitted conditional inference trees year by year on
elevation, aspect in eight classes, forest and pine cover, tree size and, from 2005, the
distance to and area of the nearest previous infestation, and found the predictors that
carried each year changed as the outbreak grew. Kunegel-Lion and Lewis (2020) fitted a
landscape logistic model on 100 m cells of a censused outbreak with northness, a soil
moisture index, distance to the infested border, emergence peak, pine cover and the
previous year's controlled and uncontrolled infestations, and found "The presence of
uncontrolled infestations in the neighbourhood the previous year is consistently a good
predictor of MPB presence" (p. 10). Chapman et al. (2012), Preisler et al. (2012), Simard et
al. (2011) and Creeden et al. (2014) fitted the same kind of model in Colorado, Oregon and
Washington, Yellowstone and the western United States, abstracts only. The present study
adopts the autologistic form with previous-year and 90 m neighbourhood terms and the
annual and sixteen-day logistic fits on standardised covariates.

Known confounds. Elevation is a composite; Wulder et al. (2006) attribute its sign to
host distribution, Nelson et al. (2007) found hot spots "almost always occur at elevations
between 800 m and 1000 m" and on warm south and west aspects early in an infestation,
abstract only, and Shore et al. (2006) review a decline of outbreak with elevation. Aspect
reads warmth as well as shade; Kaiser et al. (2012) found infestation more likely on "south-
facing slopes, steep slopes, locations with small contributing areas and locations with
lower values of the topographic wetness index", abstract only, which is the opposite of the
shading prediction. The stand inventory is projected and partly post-attack, which no prior
study using the Vegetation Resources Inventory avoided.

Prior effect sizes. Odds of red attack rose 0.7 per cent per metre of elevation and fell
15.64 per cent per unit of slope (Wulder et al. 2006, p. 159). Mortality during epidemics
rose 1.5 to 4 per cent per centimetre of diameter above about 10 cm (Shore et al. 2006, p.
101). The 25 cm sink and source threshold of Carroll and Safranyik (2004) stands. No prior
study reports a sky view or wind shelter coefficient on attack.

Where this study sits. A replication of the autologistic landscape design at 30 m with
terrain separated into named properties, and an extension in that shelter, sky view and a
flight-window wind field enter together with stand density.

## Question 2, wind through stand density

Established methods. Paired thinned and unthinned stands with microclimate sensors and
pheromone traps (Bartos and Amman 1989), partial-cutting trials with vigour measurements
(Amman et al. 1988; Mitchell et al. 1983; Waring and Pitman 1985), autologistic colonisation
models in thinned and unthinned plots (Preisler and Mitchell 1993), tracer gas plumes in
canopies (Thistle et al. 2004; Edburg et al. 2010), and stand density trials in Colorado
(Negrón 2018; Schmid and Mata 2005; Mata et al. 2003).

What was established. Bartos and Amman (1989) measured over a 23 day period around the
flight peak that "Thinning lodgepole pine stands increased light intensity, wind movement,
insolation, and temperature" and that "Fewer mountain pine beetles (5 percent of the total)
were caught in pheromone-baited traps in a thinned than in an adjacent unthinned stand.
Percentage of trees killed by mountain pine beetle was only 2 percent in a thinned stand,
compared to 16 percent in an adjacent unthinned stand" (inside front cover), and read this
as microclimate rather than vigour, because the residual trees had not yet grown. Amman et
al. (1988) found low infestation in partially cut stands despite poor growth response,
abstract only. Waring and Pitman (1985) found resistance rose with growth efficiency once
canopy density was reduced, abstract only, which is the vigour explanation. Mitchell et al.
(1983) found no mortality in heavily thinned stands, abstract only. Schmid et al. (1992)
found bark warmer by day in partially cut stands, abstract only, and Bartos and Booth
(1994) measured the temperature dynamics of thinning directly. Thistle et al. (2004) found
"Plume dilution was highest in this study in the more open ponderosa pine canopy", abstract
only. Against the mechanism, Preisler and Mitchell (1993) found "Conditional probabilities
of attack for trees near other attacked trees were actually greater in thinned plots,
indicating that the wide spacing between trees in these plots did not seem to interfere
with the switching of attacks between trees", abstract only. Negrón (2018) found "Brood
emergence was not influenced by stand density" while "The probability of individual tree
attack is influenced by stocking and tree size", abstract only. Hood et al. (2016) found
about 50 per cent of ponderosa pine killed in dense untreated stands against almost none
in thinned, abstract only.

Timescale of wind. Bartos and Amman (1989) recorded wind hourly by micrologger over 23
days. No landscape model in the screening table entered wind below the canopy; Ramazi et
al. (2021) entered a July to August mean daily station wind speed and ranked it second
among predictors at a three-year horizon (source note), and Kunegel-Lion and Lewis (2020)
read aspect through prevailing wind without measuring wind (source note).

Prior effect sizes. Trap catch 5 per cent and mortality 2 against 16 per cent (Bartos and
Amman 1989). Mortality about 50 per cent against almost none (Hood et al. 2016, abstract).

Where this study sits. An extension of the stand-scale microclimate result to a
landscape, with the interaction form the mechanism implies and the smaller effect an
unmanaged density range yields.

## Question 3, terrain, dispersal and landing

Established methods. Landing traps (Hynum and Berryman 1980), release and recapture under
the canopy (Safranyik et al. 1989, 1992), radar and aerial capture above it (Jackson et al.
2008; Ainslie and Jackson 2010), red and green attack cluster geometry (Robertson et al.
2007), sink and source patches on the provincial survey (Chen and Walton 2011), landscape
features at the expansion front (de la Giroday et al. 2011), simulation of wind-aided
flight through forest (Byers 2000), and gene flow (Janes et al. 2014).

What was established. Beetles "were unable to distinguish between hosts, dead hosts and
nonhosts during landing" (Hynum and Berryman 1980). Dispersal distances "of 30 meters and
50 meters were consistently observed" (Robertson et al. 2007, abstract). "SDD accounted for
85.3% of mountain pine beetle dispersal to non-infested areas and 96.8% of beetle dispersal
to infested areas" (Chen and Walton 2011, p. 1). Landscape features "provide impactive
surfaces for interception of insects" and settlement rises "in areas where wind speed is
reduced" (de la Giroday et al. 2011). Janes et al. (2014) read gene flow up forested river
valleys and westerly winds carrying the eastward jump (source note). Gray et al. (1972)
found emergence peaking between 11 a.m. and 2 p.m. and the upwind response weakening above
4 to 7.5 km/h (source note).

Known confounds. Sky view and the shelter index are functions of slope and aspect and so
of radiation, which is the confound the study's own third question tests.

Prior effect sizes. None reported as a terrain coefficient on landing.

Where this study sits. New work at the landscape scale, reading a shelter main effect as
deposition, with de la Giroday et al. (2011) the one prior reading and one that found the
opposite face.

## Question 4, novelty

Of the 198 included records, none read in full or by abstract entered a terrain-resolved
wind field below the canopy together with stand density in a landscape model of attack.
Two came closest. Ramazi et al. (2021) entered a seasonal mean station wind speed, and the
conference items of Jackson's group from 2004 to 2006 on topography and above-canopy
transport, which have no accessible text, led to Ainslie and Jackson (2010), which
resolved the wind over terrain above the canopy without the stand beneath it. The novelty
statement is therefore written as what this study did, with those precedents named, and
not as an absence claim.
